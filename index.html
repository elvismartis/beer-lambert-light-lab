🐍 Python Code (Reproduce Calculation & Curve) 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Beer–Lambert Law parameters from current Light Lab settings
I0 = 100.0          # Incident intensity
b = 1.00            # Path length (cm)
eps_X = 10000       # L / (mol * cm)
c_X = 20.0e-6       # mol / L
eps_Y = 5000        # L / (mol * cm)
c_Y = 0.0e-6        # mol / L

# Compute absorbances and transmittance
A_X = eps_X * b * c_X
A_Y = eps_Y * b * c_Y
A_total = A_X + A_Y
T = 10 ** (-A_total)
I = I0 * T

print(f"Absorbance: {A_total:.4f} AU")
print(f"Transmittance: {T:.4f} ({T*100:.2f}%)")
print(f"Transmitted Light (I): {I:.2f} units")
Step-by-Step Numerical Substitution
Component X: \(A_X = \varepsilon_X \cdot b \cdot c_X\) 10,000 × 1.00 × 2.00×10⁻⁵ = 0.2000
Component Y: \(A_Y = \varepsilon_Y \cdot b \cdot c_Y\) 5,000 × 1.00 × 0.00 = 0.0000
Total Absorbance: \(A_{\text{total}} = A_X + A_Y\) 0.2000 + 0.0000 = 0.2000 AU
Transmittance: \(T = 10^{-A_{\text{total}}}\) 10^(-0.2000) = 0.6310 (63.10%)
Transmitted Radiant Power: \(I = I_0 \cdot T\) 100.0 × 0.6310 = 63.10 units

The Mathematics

From measured light intensities to a concentration-dependent analytical signal.

1. Transmittance

T = I / I₀

Transmittance is the fraction of incident radiant intensity that passes through the sample without being absorbed. Percent transmittance is \(\%T = 100 \times T\).

2. Absorbance

A = −log₁₀(T) = log₁₀(I₀ / I)

Absorbance is a logarithmic measure of attenuation. It is dimensionless; "AU" (Absorbance Units) is an analytical reporting label rather than a physical unit.

3. Beer–Lambert Law

A = ε · b · c

For monochromatic radiation through an ideal homogeneous solution, absorbance is directly proportional to molar absorptivity (\(\varepsilon\)), optical path length (\(b\)), and solute concentration (\(c\)).

Unit Consistency: Molar absorptivity \(\varepsilon\) is expressed in \(\text{L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{cm}^{-1}\), path length \(b\) in \(\text{cm}\), and concentration \(c\) is converted from \(\mu\text{M}\) to \(\text{mol}\cdot\text{L}^{-1}\) (\(1\,\mu\text{M} = 10^{-6}\,\text{M}\)).

Derivation in Four Steps

Why the exponential attenuation and logarithm arise from differential optics.

1
A differential thin layer attenuates light.

Across an infinitesimal slice \(dx\), intensity loss is proportional to incident intensity, concentration, and thickness: \(-\mathrm{d}I = k I c \,\mathrm{d}x\).

2
Separate variables and integrate.

\(-\int_{I_0}^I \frac{\mathrm{d}I}{I} = k c \int_0^b \mathrm{d}x \implies \ln\left(\frac{I_0}{I}\right) = kbc\).

3
Convert to common base-10 logarithm.

Using \(\ln(u) = 2.3026 \log_{10}(u)\), we obtain \(\log_{10}\left(\frac{I_0}{I}\right) = \left(\frac{k}{2.3026}\right)bc\).

4
Define molar absorptivity \(\varepsilon\) and Absorbance \(A\).

Setting \(\varepsilon = k / 2.3026\) yields \(A = -\log_{10}(T) = \varepsilon b c\).

I = I₀ · 10−εbc
T = 10−εbc
A = εbc

Why Absorbances Add

The mathematical foundation of multi-component simultaneous spectrophotometry.

When multiple absorbing chemical species coexist without chemical reaction or spectral cross-coupling at a given wavelength, their total absorbance is strictly additive:

Atotal = AX + AY
Atotal = b(εXcX + εYcY)

In contrast, transmittances never add; their attenuation factors multiply:

Tmixture = TX × TY
Binary Analysis in Practice: By recording total absorbances at two distinct wavelengths (\(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\)), two simultaneous linear equations can be solved to determine individual concentrations \(c_X\) and \(c_Y\).

Assumptions and Limitations of the Beer–Lambert Law

The Beer–Lambert law represents a physical model with clear boundaries of validity. Real laboratory spectrophotometers encounter physical and chemical deviations:

Fundamental Assumptions

  • Monochromatic radiation: The incident beam consists of a single wavelength or a bandwidth narrower than the absorption band.
  • Homogeneous medium: Solute molecules are uniformly dispersed with negligible particulate scattering or turbidity.
  • Independent absorbers: Solute particles do not electrostatically or chemically interact with one another.
  • Parallel rays: Light rays travel along parallel trajectories perpendicular to the cuvette faces.

Real-World Deviations

  • High concentrations: At \(c > 10\,\text{mM}\), intermolecular distances diminish, perturbing charge distribution and altering \(\varepsilon\).
  • Chemical equilibria: Solute association, dissociation, tautomerization, or pH-dependent protonation changes the absorbing species.
  • Instrumental stray light: Radiation of spurious wavelengths hitting the detector causes negative deviations at high absorbances (\(A > 2.0\)).
  • Solvent absorption & refractive index: Large shifts in solute concentration alter solution refractive index, perturbing \(\varepsilon\).

Educational Note: In high-performance UV-Vis spectroscopy, standard working absorbance is typically maintained between \(0.1\) and \(1.5\,\text{AU}\) to minimise photometric detector noise and stray light errors.
