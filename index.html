The mathematics

From measured light intensities to a concentration-dependent signal.

1. Transmittance

T = I / I₀

Transmittance is the fraction of incident radiant intensity that reaches the detector. It is often reported as percent transmittance: %T = 100 × T.

2. Absorbance

A = −log₁₀(T) = log₁₀(I₀ / I)

Absorbance is a logarithmic measure of attenuation. It is dimensionless; “AU” is a commonly used reporting label, not a physical unit.

3. Beer–Lambert law

A = εbc

For monochromatic light in an ideal homogeneous absorbing solution, absorbance is proportional to molar absorptivity, path length and concentration.

The simulator uses ε in L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹, b in cm and concentration converted from µM to mol L⁻¹.

Derivation in four steps

Why the logarithm appears naturally.

1
A thin layer attenuates light.

For a small thickness dx, the intensity loss is proportional to the intensity entering the layer, concentration and thickness: −dI = kIc dx.

2
Separate variables and integrate.

−dI/I = kc dx. Integrating from I₀ to I and from 0 to b gives ln(I₀/I) = kbc.

3
Convert to common logarithm.

Since ln(x) = 2.303 log₁₀(x), log₁₀(I₀/I) = (k/2.303)bc.

4
Define ε and absorbance.

Set ε = k/2.303 and A = log₁₀(I₀/I). Therefore A = εbc.

I = I₀e−kbc
T = e−kbc
A = −log₁₀T = εbc

Why absorbances add

The principle behind classical two-component simultaneous estimation.

For independent absorbing species at the same wavelength, the mixture absorbance is the sum of their individual contributions:

Atotal = AX + AY
Atotal = b(εXcX + εYcY)

Transmittances do not add. For ideal independent absorbers, their transmittance factors multiply:

Tmixture = TX × TY
This additivity assumes no chemical interaction that changes the absorbing species, and no significant uncorrected scattering, stray light or matrix interference.

Assumptions and limitations

The Beer–Lambert law is a model with a defined domain of validity. This interactive tool is educational, not a substitute for instrument qualification or analytical method validation.

Model assumptions

  • Monochromatic or sufficiently narrow-band radiation.
  • Homogeneous, clear solution with negligible scattering.
  • Absorbing species behave independently.
  • Concentration is within a range where the response is linear.
  • Path length and absorptivity remain constant.

Real-world deviations

  • High concentrations and molecular interactions.
  • Changes in pH, ionisation or chemical speciation.
  • Stray light, finite spectral bandwidth and detector limitations.
  • Turbidity, particulates and scattering.
  • Absorption by solvents, excipients or degradation products.

Educational note: “1 − T” is the fraction of incident light not transmitted. It should not automatically be interpreted as true molecular absorption if reflection or scattering is appreciable.
